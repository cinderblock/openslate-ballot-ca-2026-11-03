p2 We, the undersigned, registered and qualified voters of the State of California, residents of the City of Newport Beach, California, pursuant to
p2 Article II, Sections I and II of the California Constitution, and sections 9200, et seq. of the California Elections Code, hereby present to the City Council
p2 of the City of Newport Beach this petition and request that the following proposed amendments to the Newport Beach General Plan be adopted by
p2 the City Council or otherwise submitted to the registered and qualified voters of the City of Newport Beach for their adoption or rejection at the next
p2 regularly scheduled municipal election or at any special election held prior to that election or as otherwise provided by law. The proposed amendments
p2 are set forth below and on the subsequent pages.
p2 The People of the City of Newport Beach do hereby ordain as follows:
p2 Section 1. Title
p2 This initiative measure shall be known as the ‘’Responsible Housing
p2 Initiative” (the “Initiative”).
p2 Section 2. Purpose and Findings.
p2 A. Purpose. This Initiative seeks to balance responsible urban development
p2 with protecting the environment, public health and safety, and the quality
p2 of life for the residents and businesses of the City of Newport Beach (the
p2 “City”). This Initiative amends the City’s General Plan to allow for more
p2 residential housing in a manner that fully satisfies, but does not unduly
p2 exceed, the requirements of California housing laws, including the State
p2 Housing Element Law and Housing Accountability Act.
p2 B. Findings. The residents of the City find that this Initiative promotes
p2 the public interest in light of its Purpose and the following facts and
p2 circumstances:
p2 1. In November 2000, City residents voted to approve the Greenlight
p2 Initiative, which added Section 423 to the Newport Beach City
p2 Charter. Section 423 prevents the Newport Beach City Council
p2 from amending the City’s General Plan to significantly increase
p2 allowed development without first securing approval from City
p2 residents. Section 423 specifically requires, among other things,
p2 that any amendment to the City’s General Plan allowing for the
p2 development of more than 100 dwelling units must be approved
p2 by a majority vote in a City election.
p2 2. When the City was preparing its 2021 - 2029 6th Cycle
p2 Housing Element (the “Housing Element”) in response to
p2 State law, the City repeatedly assured the community it
p2 would allow new development only to extent required by
p2 State law. Under State law, the City was asked to update its
p2 Housing Element to allow for 4,845 new dwelling units at
p2 varying levels of affordability (e.g., low income, moderate income).
p2 This is commonly referred to as the City’s Regional Housing Needs
p2 Allocation or “RHNA.”
p2 3. In September 2022, the City adopted the Housing Element.
p2 That Housing Element specified that implementation of the
p2 Housing Element “will require a companion Land Use
p2 Element amendment that will be subject to a vote of the
p2 electorate pursuant to Charter Section 423.” In October 2022,
p2 the California Department of Housing and Community
p2 Development (“HCD”) reviewed the City’s Housing Element and
p2 informed the City that it must implement all programs identified
p2 in the Housing Element, including “Initiating a Ballot Measure for a
p2 Charter Section 423 Vote.’’
p2 4. Between October 2022 and April 2024, City officials and staff
p2 worked to implement the Housing Element. The City stated that
p2 such implementation would require several major land use
p2 approvals, including significant amendments to the Land Use
p2 Element of the City’s General Plan, the City’s Zoning Ordinance,
p2 and the City’s Local Coastal Program. These approvals were
p2 referred to collectively as the “Housing Element Implementation
p2 Program Amendments.”
p2 5. Throughout the process to implement the Housing Element, the
p2 City consistently told residents they would have an opportunity
p2 to vote on proposed amendments to the Land Use Element, as
p2 required by Section
p2 423. Prior to July 2024, the City never suggested to voters that the
p2 Newport Beach City Council (“City Council”) would not send this
p2 issue to a vote under Charter Section 423.
p2 6. In April 2024, the Housing Element Implementation Program
p2 Amendments were presented to the Newport Beach Planning
p2 Commission for review and recommendation. The Planning
p2 Commission recommended approval of the Housing Element
p2 Implementation Program Amendments. The Resolution adopted by
p2 the Planning Commission stated that final adoption would “require
p2 a majority vote of the electorate” under Section 423. (Resolution
p2 No. PC2024- 006.)
p2 7. The Housing Element Implementation Program Amendments
p2 presented to the Planning Commission proposed allowing for
p2 development of at least 8,174 new dwelling units. That proposal
p2 reflected a 68% increase above the RHNA requirement, or 3,329
p2 excess dwelling units.
p2 8. On July 23, 2024, the City Council held a meeting purporting to
p2 approve the proposed Housing Element Implementation Program
p2 Amendments and purporting to authorize a dramatic increase
p2 in the scale, height and density of residential development
p2 in Newport Beach. The City Council also purported to adopt
p2 proposed amendments to the Land Use Element. Those purported
p2 amendments include the addition of Policy LU 4.4, which is
p2 intended to exceed the City’s RHNA through the adoption of
p2 Housing Opportunity (HO) Overlay Zoning Districts and other land
p2 use policies. Local voters did not approve of any of those purported
p2 amendments.
p2 9. During that same July 23 meeting, the City Council asserted
p2 without legal support that a Charter Section 423 vote on the
p2 proposed Land Use Element amendments was purportedly
p2 “precluded” by State law.
p2 10. On September 24, 2024, the City Council adopted–again,
p2 without voter approval–Ordinance No. 2024-16, which purports
p2 to amend the City’s Zoning Code and associated maps to add HO
p2 Overlay Zoning Districts. These HO Overlay Zoning Districts alone
p2 purport to allow for more than eight thousand additional dwelling
p2 units to be developed throughout six large areas of Newport Beach.
p2 11. The decision to adopt any major amendment authorizing
p2 thousands of additional dwelling units belongs to the voters, not
p2 the City Council.
p2 12. Unlike Newport Beach, the City of Yorba Linda chose to honor
p2 its local voter approval requirements when adopting its Housing
p2 Element implementation strategy. In November 2024, Yorba Linda
p2 voters overwhelming approved Measure JJ, a measure the Yorba
p2 Linda City Council placed on the ballot in June 2024 to comply
p2 with Yorba Linda’s “Right-to-Vote Amendment” (Measure B). Under
p2 Measure JJ, Yorba Linda will satisfy its RHNA of 2,415 units by relying
p2 on pipeline projects (181 units) and underutilized sites (569 units),
p2 and then rezoning specific properties to accommodate additional
p2 units (1,747 units). In total, Yorba Linda plans to accommodate 2,497
p2 units through its Housing Element and associated implementation
p2 program. This will result in no more than 82 units beyond what
p2 State law requires, or an approximately three percent (3%) increase
p2 above Yorba Linda’s RHNA of 2,415 units.
p2 13. Like the approach taken by the City of Yorba Linda, this Initiative
p2 would allow the City to achieve housing objectives while ensuring
p2 the local community has a voice in major land use decisions.

p5 Impartial Analysis
p5 City of Newport Beach
p5 Measure H
p5 State law requires the City of Newport Beach to maintain a Housing Element identifying adequate sites to accommodate its Regional Housing Needs
p5 Allocation by income category.
p5 If adopted, this measure would amend the City’s General Plan (Policy LU 4.4) to impose limits and affordability requirements for up to 2,900 dwelling
p5 units within five housing opportunity areas. Of those units, 2,160 would be reserved for extremely low-, very low-, and low-income households, with
p5 the remainder designated for moderate-income households. The measure also establishes a density range of 20 to 50 units per gross acre, plus any
p5 additional density permitted by state law, which may affect how residential development is distributed among sites.
p5 State housing law requires local governments to identify and address governmental constraints to housing development and to maintain sufficient
p5 capacity to meet housing obligations by income category. The measure’s affordability requirements and development limitations could constrain the
p5 City’s ability to satisfy these obligations. If the California Department of Housing and Community Development determines that the measure reduces
p5 housing capacity or constrains housing production, or if the City is otherwise required to identify additional housing capacity under state law, the City
p5 may be required to amend its Housing Element, identify additional housing sites, or take other actions to maintain compliance with state housing law.
p5 Because the measure would establish voter-approved limitations on housing locations, densities, and affordability requirements, the City’s ability to
p5 modify housing capacity, designate replacement sites or respond to future state housing requirements may be limited without voter approval. If the
p5 City is unable to maintain compliance with state housing law, it could be subject to administrative or judicial enforcement actions authorized by state
p5 law, including limitations on certain local land use authority, court-ordered remedies and monetary penalties.
p5 The measure does not approve any specific development project. Future projects would remain subject to applicable review and approval requirements.

p5 A “NO” vote is a vote against amending the General Plan.

p7 Argument Against Measure H
p7 Vote NO on Measure H. This measure mandates 2,900 units for extremely
p7 low-, very low-, low-, and moderate-income households, including 2,160
p7 units reserved for lower-income households. By restricting market-
p7 rate housing above moderate income, it pushes development into
p7 narrow income tiers, concentrating lower-income housing into five
p7 distinct communities rather than supporting integrated, mixed-income
p7 communities that provide greater housing diversity.
p7 The measure also introduces significant financial obstacles to housing
p7 development. Housing developments in high-cost jurisdictions often
p7 require substantial public subsidies, yet the measure identifies no funding
p7 source for those subsidies. Nor does it identify funding sources for
p7 roads, utilities, parks, public safety, or related infrastructure. Raising the
p7 question, how will these projects and related infrastructure be financed
p7 and who ultimately bears the cost?
p7 The measure also raises serious questions about compliance with state
p7 housing law. State law requires Newport Beach maintain a certified
p7 Housing Element with adequate housing capacity, which also addresses
p7 development constraints. By reallocating and conditioning how units are
p7 produced, the measure could alter the assumptions underlying that plan,
p7 potentially requiring revisions and risking a finding of noncompliance.
p7 Failure to maintain compliance carries real consequences, including court
p7 intervention, limits on local land use authority, and financial penalties.
p7 Furthermore, by embedding detailed affordable housing requirements
p7 in the General Plan, the measure reduces flexibility and makes future
p7 adjustments more difficult. As state laws and local needs evolve, repeated
p7 amendments may be required to remain compliant, creating uncertainty
p7 and reducing the City’s ability to respond to changing housing and
p7 infrastructure needs.
p7 Growth works best where infrastructure can support it. By imposing
p7 rigid allocation requirements, the measure may push development out
p7 of alignment with existing infrastructure and limit more flexible, mixed-
p7 income approaches.
p7 Vote NO on Measure H.
